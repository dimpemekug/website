For years, a smartphone's lifespan was decided by three things almost never printed on the box: how long the manufacturer would update the software, how long the battery would hold up, and whether fixing a cracked screen would cost less than buying a new phone. Since June 20, 2025, those factors have been legal requirements in the European Union, and a year on, the phones in stores are starting to show the effects.

## What the rules require

The EU ecodesign regulation for smartphones and tablets sets a common floor for every manufacturer selling in the Union:

- **Operating system updates for at least five years** after the model stops being sold. The clock doesn't start at launch, but when the model is taken off the market.
- **Spare parts available for seven years**, also counted from the end of sales: screen, battery, cameras, connectors, and other essential components, with defined delivery times.
- **Batteries that last at least 800 charge cycles** while keeping 80% of their original capacity.
- **Minimum durability** against accidental drops, scratches, dust, and water.
- **An energy label** that, besides consumption, shows battery endurance, drop resistance, battery cycles, and a repairability class from A to E.

## How to read the label

The label looks like the one on home appliances, and should be read the same way: not as an absolute grade, but as a way to compare two models. The most useful entries before buying are the **repairability class**, which accounts for how easy the phone is to take apart, spare part availability, and the information provided to repairers, and the **number of battery cycles**, which says a lot about how the phone will behave in its third or fourth year.

Every label carries a QR code linking to EPREL, the European product database, where the full technical sheets live.

## The problems still unsolved

1. **Spare part prices.** A part being available says nothing about what it costs. If an original battery plus labor comes close to the value of the used phone, repair stays theoretical.
2. **Software pairing of components.** Some manufacturers tie parts to a specific device, and an unauthorized replacement can lose features or trigger warnings. The regulation sets limits, but this is still contested ground with independent repairers.
3. **Apps, not just the system.** An updated phone can still be left behind by apps, which drop support for older Android or iOS versions on their own schedule.
4. **Updates don't mean new features.** The obligation covers system continuity and security, not a guarantee of getting every new feature from later models.

> **Tip:** before buying, compare the repairability class and battery cycles on the label, then look up the manufacturer's stated end-of-updates date for that model. A mid-range phone with five years of support ahead is often worth more than a flagship at the end of its cycle.

## What to expect from here

The next step comes from the EU battery regulation: from 2027, batteries in portable devices will have to be replaceable by the user, with some exceptions for particularly durable models. Together with the ecodesign rules, the expected result is a market where keeping the same phone for five or six years becomes normal, and where refurbished stops being a fallback choice. For buyers, the biggest change is also the simplest: the information needed to choose a phone that lasts is there — you just have to look at it.
