The ground shakes, and within minutes your phone fills up with numbers: magnitude 4.4, then 4.6, another source saying 4.5, someone talking about a "level V". It isn't confusion or an attempt to hide something: these are different measurements, taken at different times, of two things that aren't the same. Understanding the difference between magnitude and intensity, and knowing where to look, helps you read the news calmly instead of chasing rumours.

## Magnitude: how big the earthquake is

Magnitude measures the earthquake at its source, that is, how much energy the fault released. Each event has a single value, which doesn't depend on where you are.

The scale is logarithmic, so small differences matter a lot. According to INGV, Italy's National Institute of Geophysics and Volcanology, at the same distance a magnitude 5 earthquake produces shaking about 10 times larger than a magnitude 4, and releases about 32 times more energy. Between a 4 and a 6 the energy grows roughly a thousandfold.

There are several ways to calculate it, and the label next to the number tells you which one was used:

1. **ML, local magnitude.** This is what's usually called "Richter", and it's the first value INGV publishes.
2. **Mw, moment magnitude.** It comes from a fuller analysis of the seismic waves; for earthquakes of magnitude 3.5 and above INGV normally computes it within 6-9 minutes.
3. **Md, duration magnitude.** It's based on how long the signal lasts and is the one used, for example, by the Vesuvius Observatory for earthquakes in the Campi Flegrei area near Naples.

## Why the numbers change

The first estimate is based on data from the first few tens of seconds, from a handful of stations. INGV warns that these preliminary magnitudes can differ from the final one by as much as half a unit, and that they're replaced as soon as more data come in. The Campi Flegrei earthquake of 13 March 2025, for example, was first reported as 4.4 and then revised to 4.6.

Different sources also give different values. INGV, the European EMSC and the American USGS use their own stations and methods, and the same event can appear with a difference of one or two tenths. For earthquakes in Italy the reference is INGV, which runs the national seismic network.

## Intensity: what was felt, and where

Intensity doesn't measure the earthquake but its effects at a specific place: how people felt it, what fell over, which buildings were damaged. The same earthquake therefore has as many intensities as there are places, and they're written in Roman numerals.

1. **It depends on distance and depth.** A moderate, shallow quake close to a town can do more damage than a stronger, deeper one far from where people live.
2. **It depends on the ground and the buildings.** Soft ground amplifies shaking, and old or fragile buildings suffer worse effects from the same quake.
3. **It has different scales.** INGV uses the Mercalli-Cancani-Sieberg (MCS) scale for questionnaire responses, while field surveys after damaging earthquakes increasingly use the European Macroseismic Scale, EMS-98. Automatic shaking maps use the Modified Mercalli scale.

That's why "a level V Mercalli earthquake" isn't a measurement of the earthquake and can't be converted into magnitude: it describes what happened in one place.

## Where to check

1. **INGV for the data.** The [terremoti.ingv.it](https://terremoti.ingv.it/en) website publishes the list of events located in Italy, with magnitude, depth and updates. For Campi Flegrei, Vesuvius and Ischia there are also the Vesuvius Observatory bulletins.
2. **The "Did you feel it?" questionnaire.** On [hsit.it](https://www.hsit.it/) anyone who felt a quake can describe what they noticed: INGV uses the responses to build the intensity map.
3. **Civil protection and the local council for instructions.** What to do, where to go, which schools stay closed: this information comes from local authorities, not from seismologists.
4. **Be wary of predictions and unsourced videos.** Nobody can predict the day, place and size of an earthquake. After a quake, old videos often circulate as if they were new, or ones made from scratch with [generative AI](/en/posts/video-ai-generativa-2026/): before sharing them, look for the same news on an official source.

## What phone alerts can do

IT-alert, Italy's public warning system that sends messages to every phone in an area, has been operational since 13 February 2024 for four risks only: nuclear accidents or radiological emergencies, major accidents at industrial plants, the collapse of a large dam, and volcanic activity at Campi Flegrei, Vesuvius and Vulcano. It doesn't warn of earthquakes, and the Civil Protection Department states that at Campi Flegrei it isn't meant for bradyseism, the slow rise and fall of the ground.

The earthquake alert system built into Android, available in many countries, is not active in Italy according to the Italian outlets that checked. Apps that show earthquakes, such as [rQuake](/en/projects/rquake/), work with published data instead: they notify you once the event has been located, which is useful for knowing what happened and where, not for warning you ahead of it. rQuake also estimates the likely intensity at your location and at your saved places, and compares the same event as measured by INGV, USGS, EMSC and GFZ GEOFON; the [event details](/en/projects/rquake/docs/event-details-and-intensity/) guide explains how.

> **Tip:** the most useful thing to do with your phone comes before the quake, not after. Save 112, the European emergency number, find out on your council's website where the nearest assembly area is, and agree on a meeting point with your family: in an emergency the lines get congested and messages arrive late.

## What to keep in mind

Magnitude and intensity answer different questions: how strong the earthquake was, and what it did where you were. The first arrives within seconds and is refined over the following minutes; the second takes shape over hours and days, partly from the responses of people who felt it. Monitoring networks are getting denser and estimates faster, but earthquakes remain unpredictable: following a few reliable sources and knowing in advance what to do matters more than any notification.
