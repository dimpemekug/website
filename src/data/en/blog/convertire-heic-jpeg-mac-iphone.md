You need to upload a photo of a document to a portal, attach a picture to a form or send it to someone on a PC, and the file is rejected: unsupported format. It happens because for years the iPhone has saved photos as HEIC rather than JPEG. It's a better format in many ways, but it isn't accepted everywhere yet. The answer is to convert when you need to, without giving up the more efficient format for everything else.

## What the HEIC format is

HEIC is the file name for the HEIF format, which Apple has used by default on iPhone and iPad since 2017, with iOS 11. It compresses images more efficiently than JPEG: at the same quality the files are noticeably smaller, and there's room for things JPEG can't handle, such as 10-bit colour depth, HDR information and the multiple images of Live Photos.

The downside is compatibility. Macs, iPhones and iPads open it without trouble, and so do many recent apps; but plenty of websites, government portals, older graphics programs and some versions of Windows without the relevant extension can't read it. Video follows the same logic: the high-efficiency setting records it in HEVC instead of H.264.

## iPhone settings

You don't always need to convert by hand: the iPhone has two settings that decide what it saves and what it hands over.

1. **Capture format.** In Settings › Camera › Formats you can choose **High Efficiency** (HEIC and HEVC) or **Most Compatible** (JPEG and H.264). With the second, all new photos will be JPEG, but they'll take up more space, and some camera features require high efficiency.
2. **Transfer to a computer.** In the Photos app settings, **Transfer to Mac or PC** set to **Automatic** converts photos to a compatible format when you copy them over a cable; **Keep Originals** copies them as they are.

The two settings are independent. If a PC still receives HEIC files, check both: a system update may have changed one of them.

Many apps already convert a photo to JPEG on their own when you share it. That's why the same file sometimes goes through without a hitch and sometimes gets rejected.

## Converting on a Mac

1. **From the Finder, with Quick Actions.** Select one or more HEIC files, right-click and choose **Quick Actions › Convert Image**. Pick JPEG and a size: the Mac creates the converted copies next to the originals.
2. **With Preview.** Open the photo, choose **File › Export** and JPEG as the format, adjusting the quality with the slider. Handy for one photo at a time.
3. **From the Photos app.** Select the photos and choose **File › Export**: in the window you can pick JPEG as the file type.

For a handful of photos these are enough. When there are hundreds, or you also need to shrink them to meet a portal's size limit, you need something more.

## Converting many photos at once

[Resizo](/en/projects/resizo/), free on the Mac App Store, is built for this: drag a folder of HEIC files into the window, choose JPEG and, if needed, a size in pixels or as a percentage; before saving you can already see how much each file will weigh. Portrait photos come out the right way up, the originals are never overwritten and everything happens on the Mac.

One detail is worth knowing: by default Resizo removes metadata from the exported files, including the [GPS location](/en/posts/togliere-posizione-gps-foto-iphone-mac/). For a photo you're sending to an office or posting online that's what you want; for a personal archive you can turn on the option that keeps it. The steps are in the guide to [HEIC conversion](/en/projects/resizo/docs/heic-conversion-and-exif-privacy/).

> **Tip:** be wary of websites that convert HEIC to JPEG for free online. To do it they have to receive your photos, which are often pictures of ID documents, receipts or people. On a Mac the conversion happens without the images ever leaving the computer.

## What to keep in mind

HEIC isn't an Apple whim: it takes up less space, keeps more information and support grows every year. That's why it makes sense to leave the iPhone on high efficiency and convert only when a website or a person asks for it, rather than giving up the better format for good. With a simple habit, a folder for the JPEG copies and the originals left where they are, you get the best of both.
